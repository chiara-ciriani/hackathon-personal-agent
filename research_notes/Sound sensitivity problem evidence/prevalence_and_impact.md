# Sound sensitivity among autistic and other sound-sensitive people: prevalence and real-life impact

Notes for the Quiet Compass pitch slide. Research date: 2026-10-24. Every number below is traced to a source. Anything I couldn't verify is listed under Gaps. UK and US figures are labeled.

**Top 8 slide-ready facts**
1. About 4 in 10 autistic people have hyperacusis right now (41.4%), and about 6 in 10 have it at some point in life (60.6%). Meta-analysis, 13,000+ autistic people, Williams et al., *Ear & Hearing* 2021. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8349927/
2. 1 in 31 US 8-year-olds has an autism diagnosis (2022 data). In California the rate is 1 in 19 (53.1 per 1,000), the highest of 16 sites. CDC MMWR, April 2025. https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/74/ss/ss7402a1.htm
3. An estimated 5.4 million US adults are autistic (2.21%), including about 702,000 in California, the most of any state. CDC/Dietz et al. 2020, using 2017 modeled data. https://archive.cdc.gov/www_cdc_gov/autism/publications/adults-living-with-autism-spectrum-disorder.html
4. 5.9% of US adults, roughly 1 in 17, report sensitivity to everyday sounds. NHIS 2014, CDC MMWR 2015. https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/preview/mmwrhtml/mm6437a8.htm
5. Misophonia causes significant burden for about 18% of UK adults, nearly 1 in 5. Vitoratou et al., *PLOS ONE* 2023, n=772, UK-representative sample. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10032546/
6. 64% of autistic people avoid going to the shops, and 28% have been asked to leave a public place for reasons linked to their autism. UK, National Autistic Society survey, 2017–18; no methodology published. https://business.senedd.wales/documents/s79315/05.09.18%20Correspondence%20-%20The%20National%20Autistic%20Society%20Cymru%20to%20the%20Committee.html?CT=2
7. 79% of autistic people feel socially isolated. UK, National Autistic Society, Too Much Information campaign. https://www.autism.org.uk/what-we-do/campaign/public-understanding/too-much-information
8. Just 22% of UK autistic adults are in any kind of employment, against about 50% of disabled people and over 80% of non-disabled people. UK ONS data, 2020, via NAS (Feb 2021). https://autism.org.uk/what-we-do/news/new-data-on-the-autism-employment-gap

(Runner-up qualitative line: autistic adults name supermarkets, restaurants and cafés, public transport, high streets and shops as their most disabling sensory environments. MacLennan et al., *Autism in Adulthood* 2023. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10726197)

## How common is sound sensitivity (autism, general population, misophonia, ADHD)?

### Takeaway
Sound sensitivity is common in autism. The best meta-analysis puts current hyperacusis at about 41% of autistic people and lifetime prevalence at about 61%. Sensory differences of some kind show up in more than 90% of autistic children. In the general population, about 6% of US adults report sensitivity to everyday sounds, and about 18% of UK adults have misophonia significant enough to burden their lives. Estimates vary a lot depending on how the question is asked.

### Cited Findings
**Autism**
- Williams, Suzman & Woynaroski, "Prevalence of Decreased Sound Tolerance (Hyperacusis) in Individuals with Autism Spectrum Disorder: A Meta-analysis," *Ear & Hearing* 2021;42(5):1137–1150. 67 studies, 13,433 autistic individuals, 91 samples, Bayesian meta-analysis. — [PMC8349927](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8349927/)
  - Current prevalence from interview/questionnaire measures: **41.42%** (95% CrI 37.23–45.84). — [same](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8349927/)
  - Lifetime prevalence: **60.58%** (95% CrI 50.37–69.76). — [same](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8349927/)
  - Objective measures (behavioral observation or psychoacoustic tests) gave a lower current prevalence: **27.30%**. This is likely the source of the "27.3%" sometimes quoted for this paper. — [same](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8349927/); quoted as 27.3% by [Canadian Audiologist](https://canadianaudiologist.ca/reducing-dependence-on-attenuation-for-decreased-sound-tolerance/?output=pdf)
  - In ADI-R samples (ages 2–14), modeled prevalence rose from 35.1% at age 2 to 71.5% at age 14. Adult data were sparse (k=6), and the authors call the age trend preliminary. — [same](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8349927/)
  - The paper explicitly does not compute an odds ratio against the general population.
- Leekam, Nieto, Libby, Wing & Gould 2007, *J Autism Dev Disord* 37(5):894–910. Using the DISCO interview, they found that "more than 90%" of autistic children had sensory abnormalities across several domains. Study 2 found these differences pervasive and persistent across age and ability in both children and adults. This is a credible primary source for the "90%+" claim. — [abstract listing](https://antoniusacademie.mijn.bsl.nl/describing-the-sensory-abnormalities-of-children-and-adults-with/545396); [Cardiff ORCA](https://orca.cardiff.ac.uk/id/eprint/32912/)
- Sensory hyper- or hypo-reactivity is part of the DSM-5 autism criteria (criterion B4). This is standard and well known, but I did not fetch the DSM text in this session.

**General population (hyperacusis / decreased sound tolerance)**
- NHIS 2014, sample adult component, noninstitutionalized US civilians 18+: **5.9%** report sensitivity to everyday sounds. For context, 11.2% report tinnitus and 21.0% have trouble following conversation in background noise. Sound sensitivity varied only slightly by age. The MMWR QuickStats page doesn't give a sample size. — [CDC MMWR 64(37), 2015](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/preview/mmwrhtml/mm6437a8.htm). *Older figure (2014 data).*
- A scoping review of hyperacusis in general and special populations reports general-population prevalence ranging from **0.2% to 17.2%**, higher in women and in people with hearing disorders. The spread comes from inconsistent definitions. — [PMC8446270 (Frontiers in Neurology 2021)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8446270/)
- Other national estimates: in Sweden, 9.2% report difficulty tolerating everyday sounds and 1.9% have a physician-diagnosed hyperacusis. In a Western Australian cohort aged 45–70 (Busselton), 15.2% report hyperacusis. — [ENT & Audiology News, Hoare & Fackrell](https://www.entandaudiologynews.com/media/14520/entjf19-hoareandfackrell.pdf); [Busselton study](https://researchinformation.umcutrecht.nl/en/publications/prevalence-of-hyperacusis-and-its-relation-to-health-the-busselto/)

**Misophonia**
- Vitoratou et al. 2023, *PLOS ONE* 18(3):e0282777. UK-representative sample stratified by sex, age and ethnicity (Prolific, n=772), using the S-Five scale plus clinician interviews. **18%** have misophonia that causes significant burden. Only 13.6% had heard of misophonia, and 2.3% identified as having it. Rates were equal across sexes. — [PMC10032546](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10032546/); [Oxford press release](https://ox.ac.uk/node/8133630). *UK.*

**ADHD**
- In a Dutch study of 116 adults with ADHD (Kooij/Bijlenga group), the ADHD group reported more sensory hyper- and hyposensitivity than controls, most notably in the auditory domain. 43% of women and 22% of men reported sensory hypo- and/or hypersensitivity, counting all senses rather than sound alone. — [APSARD summary](https://apsard.org/?p=3640)
- A 2025 *Journal of Attention Disorders* study (vol. 30(1), Jan 2026, n=492 adults) found auditory hyper-reactivity clearly elevated in both ADHD and autistic adults compared with controls. It reports effect sizes, not a prevalence percentage. — [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/10870547251361226)

### Inferences
- If you compare 41% (autism) with 5.9% (US adults), autistic people are roughly 7x more likely to have sound sensitivity. The measures aren't equivalent, though. Say "many times more common," not "7x," unless the slide footnotes it as a rough comparison.
- The 18% misophonia figure and the 5.9% NHIS figure measure different things: trigger-sound distress versus loudness or everyday-sound tolerance. Don't add them together.

### Gaps
- I found no robust ADHD-specific prevalence percentage for sound sensitivity or hyperacusis alone.
- I didn't find a newer US general-population hyperacusis figure than NHIS 2014.
- I didn't verify the DSM-5 wording directly in this session. It is standard knowledge, but no URL was fetched.

## How many autistic people are there (US, California)?

### Takeaway
Diagnosed autism among US children is now 1 in 31, and 1 in 19 at the California site. Modeled estimates put US autistic adults at about 5.4 million (2.21%), with the largest state count in California.

### Cited Findings
- CDC ADDM Network, MMWR Surveillance Summary 74(2), published April 17, 2025. Data year 2022, 16 sites, 8-year-olds: **32.2 per 1,000 (1 in 31)**. Boys 49.2 and girls 14.3 per 1,000, a ratio of 3.4:1. California site: **53.1 per 1,000 (about 1 in 19)**, the highest of all sites. — [CDC MMWR](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/74/ss/ss7402a1.htm)
- Sites ranged from 1 in 103 in Laredo, Texas, to 1 in 19 in California. CDC attributes much of the variation to differences in screening and diagnostic services. — [Disability Scoop](https://disabilityscoop.com/?p=31413) (secondary)
- One secondary source reports 1 in 34 for 4-year-olds, with California at 1 in 17. I did not verify this against the MMWR. — [Pathfinders for Autism](https://pathfindersforautism.org/autism-prevalence-update-2025/)
- Dietz, Rose, McArthur & Maenner 2020, *J Autism Dev Disord* 50(12):4258–4266. Using 2017 data, about **2.21%** of US adults (95% SI 1.95–2.45%), or **5,437,988** adults aged 18–84, are estimated to be autistic. State estimates range from 1.97% (Louisiana) to 2.42% (Massachusetts). California has the most autistic adults, at **701,669**. The method projects child prevalence onto adults using Bayesian models and mortality data. No validated adult screening survey exists. — [CDC archive summary](https://archive.cdc.gov/www_cdc_gov/autism/publications/adults-living-with-autism-spectrum-disorder.html); [Disability Scoop](https://disabilityscoop.com/?p=28327). *Older (2017 data).*
- Critics argue the California adult estimate is implausibly high compared with roughly 39,000 adult autism cases served by the state Department of Developmental Services. This is an advocate's blog, not peer-reviewed. Many autistic adults are undiagnosed or don't use DDS services, which partly explains the gap. — [NCSA blog](https://www.ncsautism.org/blog/7g63q1d3zttpx9ye9u8r0hsed6fa8t)

### Inferences
- Rough audience math for an SF pitch: about 2.2% autistic adults, plus about 6% of adults sensitive to everyday sounds, plus misophonia and ADHD. The sound-sensitive population is plausibly well above 1 in 20 adults. Overlap between these groups is unknown, so present this as "millions," not as a precise sum.

### Gaps
- I didn't find an SF-specific adult autism count. The Dietz paper is state-level only.
- I didn't find an updated (post-2017) US adult prevalence estimate.

## Real-life impact: avoiding public places, overload, burnout

### Takeaway
UK survey data show most autistic people avoid shops, and more than a quarter have been asked to leave public places. Qualitative research names supermarkets, cafés, restaurants, transit and high streets as the most disabling sensory environments. That is where Quiet Compass would operate. Sensory overload is tied to meltdowns, shutdowns and autistic burnout, in which tolerance for stimuli drops further.

### Cited Findings
- **64%** of autistic people avoid going to the shops, and **28%** have been asked to leave a public place for reasons associated with their autism. National Autistic Society survey, used for Autism Hour (2017) and the Too Much Information campaign. *UK.* NAS has published no sample size or methodology, so treat these as charity survey figures. — [NAS Cymru evidence to the Welsh Senedd, 2018](https://business.senedd.wales/documents/s79315/05.09.18%20Correspondence%20-%20The%20National%20Autistic%20Society%20Cymru%20to%20the%20Committee.html?CT=2); [Retail Gazette 2017](https://www.retailgazette.co.uk/blog/2017/08/intu-calling-for-nationwide-autism-hour/)
- **79%** of autistic people feel socially isolated. NAS Too Much Information campaign, 2015–2018. *UK*, no sample size given. — [NAS campaign page](https://www.autism.org.uk/what-we-do/campaign/public-understanding/too-much-information)
- The campaign's underlying survey reached more than 7,000 autistic people and families. Only 16% thought the public meaningfully understands autism. A claim that "half of families never leave the house" appears only in secondary coverage, and I could not verify it. — [Activity Alliance](https://activityalliance.org.uk/news/1654-too-much-information-national-autistic-society-launch-major-awareness-campaign)
- NAS describes noise, smells and bright lights as painful and distressing for many autistic people, leading to overwhelm. — [NAS TMI page](https://www.autism.org.uk/what-we-do/campaign/public-understanding/too-much-information)
- MacLennan et al. 2023, "It Is a Big Spider Web of Things," *Autism in Adulthood*. 7 online focus groups, 24 autistic adults, UK. Supermarkets, eateries (restaurants and cafés), high streets, public transport, healthcare settings and retail shops were commonly disabling. Outdoor spaces, museums, concert venues, cinemas and stadiums were commonly less disabling. Sensory experiences were interconnected with social and environmental factors (the "spider web"). — [PMC10726197](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10726197)
- Raymaker et al. 2020, AASPIRE, *Autism in Adulthood*, qualitative interviews plus internet sources. They define autistic burnout as chronic life stress and a mismatch between expectations and abilities without adequate support, marked by pervasive long-term exhaustion, loss of function and **reduced tolerance to stimulus**. Summaries of the study note that it can lead to sensory overload, meltdowns, shutdowns and avoidance of previously tolerable settings. — [Thinking Autism Guide interview with Raymaker](https://thinkingautismguide.com/2019/08/autistic-burnout-interview-with.html); [NAS on autistic burnout](https://autism.org.uk/advice-and-guidance/professional-practice/autistic-burnout)
- A UK Data Service qualitative survey of 78 autistic adults on their accounts of sensory overload (Millington & Simmons, 2023) is available. — [UK Data Service](https://reshare.ukdataservice.ac.uk/856923)

### Inferences
- The places autistic adults call most disabling (cafés, restaurants, transit, busy streets) overlap heavily with what an event-goer in SF moves through. That supports a "find the quieter spot nearby" product.
- Interesting for the pitch: concert venues and stadiums were rated less disabling in MacLennan, possibly because people know what to expect and can plan. That argues for giving people predictability and information, which is what an app provides.

### Gaps
- I found no US quantitative survey on the percentage of autistic people who avoid or leave places because of noise specifically.
- The NAS 64%/28% figures have no published sample size or year. They are cited from 2017 onward.
- I found no quantitative link between sound sensitivity and anxiety or burnout rates in this pass.

## Cost: employment and isolation

### Takeaway
Employment for autistic adults is very low. In the UK, 22% are in any work. In the US, only 58% of young autistic adults had ever worked in their early 20s. Noise is consistently named as a leading workplace barrier.

### Cited Findings
- **22%** of autistic adults in the UK are in any kind of employment, against around 50% of disabled people and over 80% of non-disabled people. Source: ONS, "Outcomes for disabled people in the UK: 2020" (several national surveys), via NAS, 19 Feb 2021. *UK*, older figure. — [NAS](https://autism.org.uk/what-we-do/news/new-data-on-the-autism-employment-gap)
- The ONS 2021 release (published Feb 2022) doesn't give an autism-specific rate in what I could see. It says disabled people with autism, severe or specific learning difficulties, or mental illness had the lowest employment rates. 53.5% of all disabled people aged 16–64 were employed, against 81.6% of non-disabled people (Jul–Sep 2021). — [ONS 2021](https://www.ons.gov.uk/peoplepopulationandcommunity/healthandsocialcare/disability/articles/outcomesfordisabledpeopleintheuk/2021)
- NAS: only **16%** of autistic people are in full-time employment. *UK*, older campaign figure. — [NAS TMI page](https://www.autism.org.uk/what-we-do/campaign/public-understanding/too-much-information)
- **US:** **58%** of young adults on the autism spectrum ever worked between ages 21 and 25, the lowest of any disability group. Over 90% of young adults with emotional disturbance, speech impairment or learning disability had worked. Drexel National Autism Indicators Report 2015, NLTS2 data collected 2009. *Old data.* — [Drexel](https://drexel.edu/autisminstitute/news-events/news/2015/April/Autism-Indicators-Young-Adult-Transition/)
- Noise as a workplace barrier: in a 2023 consensus study, noise and communication differences were the main difficulties for autistic employees, and reasonable adjustments were usually not made. — [York St John repository, Petty et al.](https://ray.yorksj.ac.uk/id/eprint/7886/1/SPetty_Research%20article_Apr23.pdf)
- In a Codex/Braver survey of 220 neurodivergent employees in Ireland and the UK (industry, not peer-reviewed), **61%** named noise as their most common workplace barrier. — [ThinkBusiness](https://www.thinkbusiness.ie/articles/neurodivergent-workplace-barriers-irish-survey-codex-report/)

### Inferences
- The employment numbers are about workplaces, not events. On a Quiet Compass slide, use them sparingly as "the cost of sensory-hostile environments" context.

### Gaps
- I found no current (2024–2026) US unemployment rate for autistic adults from an authoritative source. The often-quoted "85% unemployed" figure has no traceable primary source and should not be used.
- I didn't find a peer-reviewed quantitative US study giving the percentage of autistic workers who cite open offices or noise as a barrier.

## Do quiet spaces, sensory accommodations or headphones help?

### Takeaway
The evidence is real but small and mostly preliminary. Noise-cancelling headphones and earmuffs show modest benefits in small studies. The retail "quiet hour" movement shows demand, but I found no outcome data for it.

### Cited Findings
- A pilot study of 21 autistic children aged 4–16 had them wear earmuffs and noise-cancelling headphones for 2 weeks each in random order, after a baseline period. Behavior improved in 5 children during the NC headphone period, but the difference from baseline was not statistically significant (p=.091). 4 children refused to wear either. — [PMC6091992](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6091992) (likely Ikuta et al. 2016, *Hong Kong J Occup Ther*; authorship not confirmed in this session)
- Pfeiffer and colleagues (Temple University) reported that autistic children participated better in community settings while wearing noise-attenuating headphones. The INSAR 2019 abstract notes that "minimal research" supports headphone effectiveness. — [Temple CPH news](https://cph.temple.edu/about/news-events/news?page=35); [INSAR 2019 abstract](https://insar.confex.com/insar/2019/webprogram/Paper30724.html)
- NAS ran a UK-wide "Autism Hour" from 2017: retailers dimmed lights and cut noise for an hour, explicitly in response to the 64% shop-avoidance figure. Intu shopping centres and Superdrug took part. — [Retail Gazette](https://www.retailgazette.co.uk/blog/2017/09/superdrug-intu-shopping-centres-autism-hour/)

### Inferences
- Headphones help some people but are refused or not enough for others. Avoiding noise in the first place, by knowing where the quiet spots are, complements them. That is a reasonable framing for Quiet Compass, though it's an inference, not a finding.

### Gaps
- I found no controlled study measuring outcomes of quiet rooms at events or venues (e.g., stadium sensory rooms) in this pass.
- I found no outcome data on retail quiet hours.

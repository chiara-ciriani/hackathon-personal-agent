# The information gap: how places sound right now, and what exists today

Researched 2026-10-24. Many sources are 2012 to 2019. The age of each claim is flagged inline. Items marked UNVERIFIED have only a secondary or search-snippet source and should not go on a slide without checking.

**Top 8 slide-ready facts**
1. Manhattan restaurants averaged 78 dBA and bars 81 dBA at prime time. About 71% of restaurants and 90% of bars were too loud for conversation (SoundPrint, 1,800+ venues, 2015-2017). https://acoustics.org/3aaa1-are-restaurants-and-bars-in-new-york-city-too-loud-gregory-scott/
2. 31% of those restaurants and 60% of those bars measured at levels potentially harmful to hearing. https://acoustics.org/3aaa1-are-restaurants-and-bars-in-new-york-city-too-loud-gregory-scott/
3. Noise was San Francisco diners' No. 1 complaint in Zagat's 2015 SF Survey (28%, ahead of service at 25%; the percentages come from secondary coverage). https://zagat.googleblog.com/2014/12/kokkari-estiatorio-takes-top-food.html
4. Google's "Popular times" and "Live busyness" are built from opted-in Location History visits. They measure crowds, not sound. https://support.google.com/business/answer/6263531
5. Yelp's noise level is a static label such as "average," and the business sets it, not diners. https://www.aarp.org/health/conditions-treatments/noisy-restaurants-hearing-loss/
6. The SF Chronicle critic dropped decibel ratings after one night's measurements in a single restaurant gave "five widely different results" depending on crowd, music and spot (2019). https://preview-prod.w.sfchronicle.com/food/restaurants/article/Are-restaurant-noise-ratings-useless-14432862.php
7. In a 4,104-person sample, 60.1% of autistic people currently had auditory sensory over-responsivity, and 71.1% had it at some point (2022, J Autism Dev Disord). https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10803-022-05747-0
8. KultureCity reports 7,000+ sensory-certified venues and events. Certification means trained staff, sensory bags and a quiet zone. It does not say how loud the room is right now. https://www.kulturecity.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/26-004-Impact-Document_V6.pdf

## Q1. Do mainstream maps/review apps show how loud a place is right now?

### Takeaway
No. Google shows crowd levels inferred from location data, and Yelp and OpenTable show static labels from businesses or reviewers. I found no evidence that Google Maps has a sensory or noise attribute. None of these platforms offers a live measured sound level.

### Cited Findings
- Google Popular times, live busyness, visit duration and wait times come from "aggregated and anonymized data from users who have opted in to Google Location History." Live busyness is "updated in real time and overlaid on the popular times graph." The help page never mentions sound or noise. — [Google Business Profile Help](https://support.google.com/business/answer/6263531)
- Google Maps accessibility attributes cover wheelchair-accessible entrance, restroom, seating, parking and elevator, plus an assistive hearing loop attribute. I found no "quiet," "sensory" or noise attribute. — [Google Maps Help](https://support.google.com/maps/answer/9882117); [Places SDK GMSPlaceAccessibilityOptions](https://developers.google.com/maps/documentation/places/ios-sdk/reference/objc/Classes/GMSPlaceAccessibilityOptions); [Google blog](https://blog.google/products/maps/how-anyone-can-make-maps-more-accessible/)
- Yelp lists noise level as a restaurant attribute, "but only in a characterization such as 'average'; also, that label is assigned by the business, not customers" (AARP, 2018). — [AARP](https://www.aarp.org/health/conditions-treatments/noisy-restaurants-hearing-loss/)
- Yelp began showing noise levels with its ratings by 2012. — [White Hutchinson (2012)](https://www.whitehutchinson.com/news/lenews/2012/july/article108.shtml)
- OpenTable let reviewers rate restaurants "quiet," "moderate" or "energetic" (2012 report). — [White Hutchinson (2012)](https://www.whitehutchinson.com/news/lenews/2012/july/article108.shtml)
- Venue managers' self-reported sound levels generally came in below measured levels (from a summary of the SoundPrint NYC study). — [summary via search of Wang/Scott paper](https://works.bepress.com/lilymwang/71) (UNVERIFIED: I didn't read the full text)

### Inferences
- Every mainstream signal is either a crowd proxy (Google) or a static, self-reported label (Yelp, OpenTable). Neither tells a sound-sensitive person how loud a room is at the moment they want to go.
- Busyness is related to noise but isn't the same thing. Music, acoustics and event type can make a half-empty room loud. See the Chronicle/Meyer Sound finding under Q4.

### Gaps
- I couldn't fetch Yelp's current (2026) business-attribute documentation, so the label set (Quiet/Average/Loud/Very Loud) and who sets it today are unverified.
- I didn't check whether Apple Maps or OpenTable show noise in 2026. Nothing came up in search.
- I didn't verify whether Google Maps added any 2025-2026 sensory attributes. Search found none.

## Q2. Evidence that venues are loud

### Takeaway
Measured data from NYC shows most restaurants and nearly all bars are too loud for conversation at prime time. In San Francisco, noise has topped diner complaints in Zagat surveys, but I found no published SF-wide SoundPrint average.

### Cited Findings
- SoundPrint NYC study: 1,800+ Manhattan restaurants and bars, each measured at least 3 times, July 2015 to June 2017, Wed-Sat about 7-9 PM. Restaurants averaged 78 dBA and bars 81 dBA. About 71% of restaurants and 90% of bars were too loud for conversation, and about 31% of restaurants and 60% of bars were potentially harmful to hearing. In some neighborhoods (Flatiron, Gramercy, East Village, LES), up to 70% of venues were in the harmful range, with averages up to 82 dBA. — [Acoustical Society of America, 174th meeting lay paper](https://acoustics.org/3aaa1-are-restaurants-and-bars-in-new-york-city-too-loud-gregory-scott/)
  - Sample sizes conflict across versions: 1,800+, 2,250 and 2,376 venues. — [ASA](https://acoustics.org/3aaa1-are-restaurants-and-bars-in-new-york-city-too-loud-gregory-scott/); [SonicField library listing](https://labs.sonicfield.org/library/analyses-of-crowd-sourced-sound-levels-logged-from-more-than-2250-restaurants-an)
- Seattle and Portland venues averaged 77 dB, and 56% of Seattle bars and restaurants were too loud for conversation (SoundPrint data reported in 2019). The same article says the loudest dining rooms are usually in NYC and San Francisco. — [Restaurant Hospitality](https://www.restaurant-hospitality.com/operations/deafening-diners-noisiest-restaurants-america)
- SoundPrint's quiet threshold is about 70 dBA or below. A third-party guideline gives Quiet ≤70, Moderate 71-75, Loud 76-80 and Very Loud 81+ dB. — [Amplifon](https://www.amplifonusa.com/hearing-loss/blog/find-quiet-environment); [Sunset](https://www.sunset.com/food-wine/restaurants/how-to-find-quiet-bars-and-restaurants)
- SF venue examples from SoundPrint readings cited in 2022: Steins 69 dB, Chili House 91 dB. — [Disquiet (2022)](https://disquiet.com/2022/11/11/finding-quiet-places-with-the-soundprint-app/)
- Zagat 2015 San Francisco Survey (published Dec 2014) named noise diners' "Top Complaint." — [Zagat blog](https://zagat.googleblog.com/2014/12/kokkari-estiatorio-takes-top-food.html). Secondary coverage gives noise 28% vs service 25% (UNVERIFIED: I didn't see the percentages on the Zagat page itself).
- Nationally, Zagat surveys ranked noise No. 2 behind service in several years (2008, 2012, 2015). In Zagat's 2015 national survey, Portland, OR diners named noise the top complaint (31%). Consumer Reports data put noise at 26% of complaints vs service at 18%. — [White Hutchinson](https://www.whitehutchinson.com/news/lenews/2012/july/article108.shtml); [amNY](https://www.amny.com/lifestyle/new-yorkers-hate-noise-but-love-going-out-to-restaurants-1-6534028); [The Pitch KC](https://www.thepitchkc.com/?p=29688)
- Hong Kong restaurants measured 66.7-82.6 dBA (mean 73.9 dBA), and variation in level shrank as occupancy density rose. — [Macao Polytechnic University research portal](https://research.mpu.edu.mo/en/publications/restaurant-noise-levels-and-temporal-characteristics/)
- The NIOSH recommended exposure limit is 85 dBA over 8 hours. The SF Chronicle says several Bay Area restaurants and bars exceed it but gives no figures. — [SF Chronicle (2019)](https://preview-prod.w.sfchronicle.com/food/restaurants/article/Are-restaurant-noise-ratings-useless-14432862.php)

### Inferences
- The typical prime-time restaurant (about 78 dBA) is well above the roughly 70 dBA conversation-friendly line. For someone with auditory over-responsivity, the median venue is a problem, not just the outliers.

### Gaps
- I found no published SF-specific SoundPrint average or percent-too-loud figure. The search result citing a "Bay Area survey 59-80 dBA" had no traceable source, so I excluded it.
- All of the NYC numbers date from 2015-2017. I found no newer large-scale venue survey.

## Q3. Existing solutions and where they fall short

### Takeaway
Current tools split into three groups. Crowdsourced decibel apps are sparse, mostly restaurants, and show historical readings. Sensory certifications and sensory hours are static and venue-run. Personal coping tools like headphones blunt the noise but don't help choose a place. None answers "where nearby is quiet right now, and how fresh is that reading?"

### Cited Findings

**SoundPrint (crowdsourced decibel app)**
- How it works: tap SoundCheck, the phone measures for 15 seconds, then you pick the venue and submit. Only iPhones and Samsung Galaxy S phones get a decibel reading, and other Android users rate by ear. Quiet or Moderate readings can nominate a venue to the Quiet List, and Loud readings can file a noise complaint. The site lists 1,600+ cities and 150+ countries with at least one measurement, but Quiet Lists for only 17 cities (NYC 52 places, London 38, Chicago 28). The site has a "Measured in the Last Few Hours" feed, so it appears active as of 2026-10. — [SoundPrint](https://www.soundprint.co/)
- 25,000+ submissions, mostly in NYC, Chicago and SF (older figure, date unclear). — [Tasting Table](https://www.tastingtable.com/695152/soundprint-restaurant-app/)
- Gap: coverage depends on volunteers visiting a venue. Readings are tied to past visits, and the service is built around restaurants and bars (inference from the sources above).

**Smartphone noise mapping research (NoiseCapture, NoiseTube)**
- Smartphone data can lack calibration or have bad location, and calibration "may lead to a high volume of workload when the number of phones increases." Phones in pockets or bags give large errors. Data only arrives when citizens actively measure, which is hard to sustain. — [NoiseCapture database paper, PMC8345695](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8345695/); [PMC9697211](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9697211/); [PLOS ONE 2016](https://www.doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0161835)
- Gap: these projects map outdoor environmental noise for researchers and city policy. They don't recommend indoor places to individuals.

**KultureCity Sensory Inclusive certification**
- 7,000+ certified venues and events. The impact document says 40 countries in one place and 5 continents in another. — [KultureCity 2026 Impact Document](https://www.kulturecity.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/26-004-Impact-Document_V6.pdf); earlier counts were 1,800+ (2024) and 250+ (older) — [theworldwar.org](https://theworldwar.org/news/museum-and-memorial-becomes-inclusive-visitors-sensory-needs); [Reviewed](https://reviewed.com/headphones/features/this-organization-is-working-to-make-more-events-inclusive-for-people-with-autism-ptsd-and-more)
- What certification includes: staff sensory training, sensory bags (noise-cancelling headphones, fidgets, cue cards, weighted lap pads), and signage for quiet zones. In 2025, 25 Live Nation venues were certified. — [Live Nation](https://news.livenationentertainment.com/news/live-nation-and-kulturecity-launch-largest-sensory-accessibility-partnership-in-live-entertainment); [TriplePundit 2025](https://www.triplepundit.com/story/2025/sensory-inclusive-events-kulturecity/824941)
- SF example: Chase Center is certified (all full-time and most part-time staff trained). Its sensory room debuted in late 2019, described as the first in the SF area, and was renovated in 2023 as the Simu Liu room. Sensory bags are at kiosks near Portals 13 and 52. — [CBS SF](https://www.cbsnews.com/sanfrancisco/news/marvel-star-simu-liu-warriors-unveils-upgraded-sensory-room-at-chase-center/); [Arena Digest 2019](https://arenadigest.com/2019/12/26/chase-center-wellness-and-sensory-room-unveiled/); [KultureCity](https://kulturecity.org/?p=1567)
- Bay Area: Oakland Museum of California is certified (first in Oakland). — [OMCA](https://museumca.org/press/certified-kulturecity-sensory-inclusive-organization/)
- Gap: certification is static and per-venue. It doesn't report current sound levels, and the sensory room is one room inside a venue that is often very loud.

**Noise-cancelling headphones**
- Studies of autistic children reported better focus and calm, and possibly less hyperstimulation. A 2021 review of 13 studies found mixed results. In a survey, 40% of speech pathologists, audiologists and teachers thought the headphones may help participation, and some worried about stigma and missing speech or instructions. — [Southern Cross University 2024](https://www.scu.edu.au/news/2024/tc-noise-cancelling-headphones-for-neurodivergent/); [ASHA LSHSS 2020](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/10.1044/2020_LSHSS-19-00119)
- Gap: headphones reduce exposure but don't help a person choose a place. Evidence on adults is thin, and fatigue and comfort complaints are anecdotal only.

### Inferences
- Each existing option covers one piece: measurement (SoundPrint, NoiseCapture), venue preparedness (KultureCity), or personal mitigation (headphones). None combines fresh, place-level sound data with a nearby recommendation and a re-check on arrival.

### Gaps
- I couldn't confirm KultureCity certification for Oracle Park, SFO, the Exploratorium or the California Academy of Sciences, and search returned nothing. The KultureCity app or directory is the source to check.
- Not researched for lack of time: NIOSH SLM app, iHEARu, AMC sensory-friendly films, retail sensory hours, Hidden Disabilities Sunflower, Autism Double-Checked, venue sensory maps, and SONYC. I have no sourced claims for these.
- I couldn't fetch the full text of the 2022 aSOR paper (paywall). The 60.1% and 71.1% figures come from the search summary of the abstract (UNVERIFIED in full text).

## Q4. Why static information fails: sound changes by hour and event

### Takeaway
Repeated readings in the same restaurant on the same night can differ widely, and occupancy drives the level. A one-time label or historical average can't tell you what the room sounds like now.

### Cited Findings
- SF Chronicle critic Soleil Ho (Sep 2019) dropped the paper's "bell-to-bomb" noise ratings. Repeated measurements in one night gave "five widely different results" depending on crowd, music and location. Meyer Sound's John Meyer says SPL meters are a poor proxy for speech intelligibility, since rooms with similar readings can feel very different. — [SF Chronicle](https://preview-prod.w.sfchronicle.com/food/restaurants/article/Are-restaurant-noise-ratings-useless-14432862.php)
- The Hong Kong restaurant study tied level and its variation to occupancy density. — [MPU](https://research.mpu.edu.mo/en/publications/restaurant-noise-levels-and-temporal-characteristics/)
- Iowa college-town worker exposure was louder on weekends and in the fall semester than on weekdays and in summer. A separate 2013 study found weekday/weekend differences, with 30-minute averages ranging from 46 to 76.7 dBA. — [Univ. of Iowa](https://iro.uiowa.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/Occupational-Noise-Exposure-of-Employees-at/9983997491502771); [AIHce 2013 slides](https://oehsai.org.uiowa.edu/sites/oehsai.org.uiowa.edu/files/2024-07/DGreen_AIHce-2013FINAL.pdf)
- The SoundPrint NYC study measured only Wed-Sat 7-9 PM, so its averages describe prime time, not the whole day. — [ASA](https://acoustics.org/3aaa1-are-restaurants-and-bars-in-new-york-city-too-loud-gregory-scott/)

### Inferences
- A 46-77 dBA range within one study suggests the same venue type can be conversation-friendly or not depending on when you go. That supports showing a timestamp and the age of each reading, and re-checking on arrival.

### Gaps
- I found no study that tabulates dBA by hour of day for venues, and none on event or conference spaces (expo halls, keynote rooms).

## Q5. Prior work combining crowdsourced sound and recommendation

### Takeaway
Academic crowdsourced noise work (NoiseTube, NoiseCapture) builds city noise maps, and SoundPrint lists quiet venues. I found no sourced example of a system that recommends a quieter nearby place from fresh readings for sound-sensitive people.

### Cited Findings
- The NoiseCapture/NoiseTube literature focuses on environmental noise maps and data quality (calibration, anomalies, participation). — [PMC8345695](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8345695/); [MDPI Sensors 2022](https://www.mdpi.com/1424-8220/22/22/8832); [VUB NoiseTube](https://soft.vub.ac.be/soft/node/383)
- Dedicated sensor networks are limited by sensor count and cost, which is the stated reason to crowdsource. — [PMC9697211](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9697211/)

### Inferences
- Quiet Compass's distinct elements: on-device short samples, timestamped freshness, agent-queried (MCP) recommendation, and arrival re-check. None of these turned up in the prior work I found.

### Gaps
- I didn't search for SONYC, "quiet routes" or "chatty maps" (Quercia et al.) projects, so their limits aren't covered here.

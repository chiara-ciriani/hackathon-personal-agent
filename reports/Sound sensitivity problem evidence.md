# Millions need quiet, and nobody can find it

Sound sensitivity is common, it shapes where people can go, and no mainstream tool tells you how loud a place is right now. The best meta-analysis finds that **41% of autistic people currently have hyperacusis and about 61% have it at some point in life**. Another **5.9% of all US adults** report sensitivity to everyday sounds, and misophonia causes significant burden for **18% of UK adults**. The cost shows up in behavior. UK surveys report that **64% of autistic people avoid shops** and **28% have been asked to leave a public place**, and the places autistic adults call most disabling are supermarkets, cafés, restaurants, transit and high streets. San Francisco makes this worse. Reported noise complaints to 311 are up **90% year over year in 2026**, and the city routinely packs 40,000 to 75,000 people into a single conference or festival. Google measures crowds, not sound. Yelp shows a static label the business sets itself. Crowdsourced decibel apps are sparse and backward-looking, and sensory rooms help only people already inside one venue. The evidence for the problem is strong. The weak spots are that the impact surveys are mostly British and that several SF-specific numbers measure complaints rather than decibels, so a pitch should phrase them carefully.

## Four in ten autistic people live with hyperacusis right now

The anchor number comes from Williams, Suzman and Woynaroski's 2021 Bayesian meta-analysis in *Ear & Hearing*: 67 studies, 91 samples, 13,433 autistic people. Interview and questionnaire measures put current hyperacusis at **41.4% (95% CrI 37.2–45.8)** and lifetime prevalence at **60.6%** ([Williams et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8349927/)). Objective measures such as behavioral observation and psychoacoustic tests gave a lower **27.3%**, which explains why that figure sometimes gets quoted for the same paper. In children the modeled rate climbed from 35% at age 2 to 72% at age 14, though the authors call the age trend preliminary because adult data were thin. Sensory differences in general are close to universal in autism: Leekam and colleagues found sensory abnormalities in **more than 90%** of autistic children, persisting across age and ability ([Cardiff ORCA](https://orca.cardiff.ac.uk/id/eprint/32912/)). A 2022 study of 4,104 autistic people reported auditory over-responsivity at 60.1% current and 71.1% lifetime ([J Autism Dev Disord](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10803-022-05747-0)). Only the abstract was checked, though, so the meta-analysis is the safer citation.

The autistic population behind those percentages is large and growing. CDC surveillance of 2022 data puts diagnosed autism at **1 in 31 US eight-year-olds**, and the California site had the highest rate of all 16 sites at **1 in 19** ([CDC MMWR 2025](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/74/ss/ss7402a1.htm)). For adults, CDC-modeled estimates from 2017 data come to **5.4 million autistic US adults (2.21%)**, with California holding the most of any state at about **702,000** ([CDC archive, Dietz et al. 2020](https://archive.cdc.gov/www_cdc_gov/autism/publications/adults-living-with-autism-spectrum-disorder.html)). That adult figure projects childhood prevalence forward and isn't a headcount. It is still the best available estimate. No reliable San Francisco count exists, but the signals point upward: SFUSD reports a "growing population of autistic students" and is expanding autism-focused classes ([SFUSD](https://www.sfusd.edu/es/node/20240)).

Autism is only part of the audience. The 2014 National Health Interview Survey found **5.9% of US adults, about 1 in 17**, report sensitivity to everyday sounds ([CDC MMWR 2015](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/preview/mmwrhtml/mm6437a8.htm)). A UK-representative study using the S-Five scale and clinician interviews found that **18%** of adults have misophonia that significantly burdens their lives, though only 13.6% had ever heard the word ([Vitoratou et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10032546/)). ADHD research consistently shows more auditory hyper-reactivity than in controls ([J Atten Disord 2025](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/10870547251361226)), but no study gives a clean ADHD-specific prevalence figure for sound sensitivity. These groups overlap by unknown amounts, and NHIS (loudness tolerance) and the misophonia study (trigger-sound distress) measure different things, so the honest framing is "millions of Americans," not a precise sum. Comparing 41% against 5.9% suggests autistic people are several times more likely to have sound sensitivity. The measures differ, though, so "many times more common" is defensible and "7x" is not.

## Noise pushes people out of shops, cafés and jobs

The cleanest impact numbers come from the UK's National Autistic Society. Its surveys, which reached more than 7,000 autistic people and families, found that **64% of autistic people avoid going to the shops** and **28% have been asked to leave a public place** for reasons linked to their autism ([NAS evidence to the Welsh Senedd, 2018](https://business.senedd.wales/documents/s79315/05.09.18%20Correspondence%20-%20The%20National%20Autistic%20Society%20Cymru%20to%20the%20Committee.html?CT=2)), and **79% feel socially isolated** ([NAS Too Much Information](https://www.autism.org.uk/what-we-do/campaign/public-understanding/too-much-information)). NAS never published sample sizes per question or full methods, so these are charity survey figures. They are widely cited and carry real weight, but they aren't peer-reviewed. No comparable US survey of noise-driven avoidance turned up.

Qualitative research shows where it hurts. In focus groups with 24 autistic adults, MacLennan and colleagues found supermarkets, restaurants and cafés, public transport, high streets and retail shops were the most commonly disabling sensory environments ([Autism in Adulthood 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10726197)). Concert venues, cinemas and stadiums were often rated less disabling, which surprised the researchers. A plausible reading is that people know what to expect at a concert and can plan for it, while a café's loudness is unpredictable. That gives a product like Quiet Compass a direct argument: predictability itself reduces harm. The stakes go beyond comfort. AASPIRE's research defines autistic burnout as long-term exhaustion and loss of function with **reduced tolerance to stimulus** ([Raymaker interview](https://thinkingautismguide.com/2019/08/autistic-burnout-interview-with.html); [NAS on burnout](https://autism.org.uk/advice-and-guidance/professional-practice/autistic-burnout)). Overload compounds: each bad environment makes the next one harder.

Employment shows the cost of sensory-hostile spaces. UK government data via NAS show just **22% of autistic adults in any employment**, against about 50% of disabled people and over 80% of non-disabled people ([NAS 2021](https://autism.org.uk/what-we-do/news/new-data-on-the-autism-employment-gap)). In the US, only **58%** of young autistic adults had ever worked between ages 21 and 25, the lowest of any disability group ([Drexel 2015](https://drexel.edu/autisminstitute/news-events/news/2015/April/Autism-Indicators-Young-Adult-Transition/)), though that data dates from 2009. Noise is named as a leading barrier. A consensus study found noise and communication differences were the main difficulties for autistic employees ([Petty et al. 2023](https://ray.yorksj.ac.uk/id/eprint/7886/1/SPetty_Research%20article_Apr23.pdf)), and in an industry survey of 220 neurodivergent workers in Ireland and the UK, **61% named noise as their top workplace barrier** ([ThinkBusiness](https://www.thinkbusiness.ie/articles/neurodivergent-workplace-barriers-irish-survey-codex-report/)). These are workplace numbers, not event numbers, so they belong on a slide as context, not as the core claim. Avoid the oft-repeated "85% of autistic adults are unemployed." It has no traceable primary source.

## San Francisco's noise complaints nearly doubled in 2026

The city's own data show reported noise at a record high. Live queries of DataSF on October 24, 2026 count **17,542 noise complaints to 311 so far this year, up 90% from 9,211 at the same point in 2025** ([DataSF 311](https://data.sf.gov/resource/vw6y-z8j6.json)). The yearly count has risen every year since 2021: 7,324, then 8,259 in 2023, 9,864 in 2024, 11,893 in 2025. Police dispatch logged another **14,230 "noise nuisance" calls** year to date ([DataSF CAD](https://data.sf.gov/resource/2zdj-bwza.json)), bringing combined reports to about **31,800, or roughly 107 a day**. The **Tenderloin, the Mission and SoMa account for 37%** of 2026 complaints, and SoMa is where Moscone Center hosts Dreamforce and most big conferences. 311 even has a dedicated "major event venue" category, which logged **831 complaints in 2026, already more than all of 2025 (661)**, with Mission Bay, home of Chase Center, among the top areas.

One caveat matters a lot. 311 renamed its noise category in 2024, and **68% of 2026 reports came through the mobile app**, so part of the jump likely reflects easier reporting. Complaints also measure annoyance and willingness to report, not decibels. On a slide, say "reported noise complaints," never "noise levels doubled." Police noise calls have actually fallen since their 2020 peak, which hints that some complaints shifted from police to 311.

Direct measurements in SF are sparse and old, and that scarcity is part of the argument. BART has been measured at **95 dB in parts of the Transbay Tube** in 2003 ([Bay Crossings](https://www.baycrossings.com/bart-quietly-makes-repairs/)) and **86–88 dB in underground segments** in a 2019 KALW test ([KALW](https://www.kalw.org/show/crosscurrents/2019-03-26/will-bart-damage-my-hearing)), above the 85 dB eight-hour occupational limit. No newer BART figure exists. Outside SFO's aircraft monitors, the city has no open network of acoustic sensors. Data that does carry decibels is either an airport-only feed, uncalibrated phone readings from NoiseCapture, environmental impact reports buried in PDFs, or a federal noise model that its own publisher says not to use for specific locations ([BTS](https://www.bts.gov/geospatial/national-transportation-noise-map)). A 2007 peer-reviewed study estimated that about 17% of San Franciscans were at risk of high annoyance from traffic noise ([Seto et al. 2007](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1914342)), but that figure is old and measures annoyance, not exposure.

Crowds are where overload happens, and SF's crowds are huge. Dreamforce 2025 drew **over 40,000 people in person** to Moscone ([SalesforceBen](https://www.salesforceben.com/dreamforce/)), and Outside Lands expected **about 75,000 a day** in Golden Gate Park in August 2026 ([GrowSF](https://growsf.org/news/2026-08-06-outside-lands-expands-muni-and-bay-wheels-service/)). Restaurants are a problem too. Noise topped San Francisco diners' complaints in Zagat's 2015 SF survey ([Zagat blog](https://zagat.googleblog.com/2014/12/kokkari-estiatorio-takes-top-food.html)). The often-quoted split of 28% noise to 25% service comes only from secondary coverage.

## Every existing tool answers a different question

The core gap is that no mainstream tool reports **how loud a place is right now**. Google's "Popular times" and "Live busyness" come from opted-in Location History visits ([Google Business Profile Help](https://support.google.com/business/answer/6263531)). They measure crowds, and Google Maps' accessibility attributes cover wheelchair access and hearing loops but nothing about sensory load or noise ([Google Maps Help](https://support.google.com/maps/answer/9882117)). Crowding and noise are related but different: music, hard surfaces and event type can make a half-empty room loud. Yelp's noise attribute is a static label such as "average," and **the business sets it, not diners** ([AARP 2018](https://www.aarp.org/health/conditions-treatments/noisy-restaurants-hearing-loss/)). When venue managers self-report sound levels, they tend to come in lower than measured levels.

Static labels fail because sound swings within a single evening. In 2019 the San Francisco Chronicle's restaurant critic dropped the paper's noise ratings after repeated readings in one restaurant on one night gave **"five widely different results"** depending on crowd, music and where she sat ([SF Chronicle](https://preview-prod.w.sfchronicle.com/food/restaurants/article/Are-restaurant-noise-ratings-useless-14432862.php)). Measured data shows how loud the baseline is. SoundPrint's study of 1,800+ Manhattan venues found restaurants averaging **78 dBA and bars 81 dBA at prime time**, with **71% of restaurants and 90% of bars too loud for conversation** and 31% of restaurants potentially harmful to hearing ([Acoustical Society of America](https://acoustics.org/3aaa1-are-restaurants-and-bars-in-new-york-city-too-loud-gregory-scott/)). That's NYC, 2015 to 2017, and other reports of the same dataset give different venue counts. Industry coverage says SF's dining rooms rank among the country's loudest ([Restaurant Hospitality](https://www.restaurant-hospitality.com/operations/deafening-diners-noisiest-restaurants-america)), but no SF-wide average has been published. For a sound-sensitive person, the typical venue is the problem, not only the outliers.

The tools that do exist each cover one slice:

| Tool | What it does | What it misses |
|---|---|---|
| SoundPrint | 15-second crowdsourced decibel readings; Quiet Lists in 17 cities ([SoundPrint](https://www.soundprint.co/)) | Depends on volunteers having visited; readings are historical; restaurants and bars only; full decibel readings on iPhone and some Samsung phones only |
| NoiseCapture / NoiseTube | Research-grade citizen noise maps ([PMC8345695](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8345695/)) | Outdoor maps for researchers and policy, not recommendations; calibration and participation problems |
| KultureCity certification | 7,000+ sensory-certified venues and events: staff training, sensory bags, quiet zones ([KultureCity 2026](https://www.kulturecity.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/26-004-Impact-Document_V6.pdf)) | Static and per-venue; says nothing about how loud the room is now; a sensory room is optional |
| SF sensory rooms | Chase Center (since 2019), Oracle Park, SFO Terminal 1 (Dec 2024) ([CBS SF](https://www.cbsnews.com/sanfrancisco/news/exclusive-chase-center-is-first-sensory-inclusive-venue-in-bay-area); [SF Autism Society](https://sfautismsociety.org/sfo-opens-first-sensory-room/)) | Help only people already inside that building; one room in a very loud venue |
| Noise-cancelling headphones | Reduce exposure; some benefit in small studies ([ASHA 2020](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/10.1044/2020_LSHSS-19-00119)) | Don't help you choose a place; a 21-child pilot found no significant behavior change and 4 children refused them ([PMC6091992](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6091992)) |

The retail "quiet hour" movement shows demand. NAS launched Autism Hour in 2017 in direct response to the 64% shop-avoidance figure ([Retail Gazette](https://www.retailgazette.co.uk/blog/2017/09/superdrug-intu-shopping-centres-autism-hour/)). But quiet hours are scheduled, not live, and no outcome data has been published for them. The research turned up no system that combines fresh, place-level sound readings with a recommendation for somewhere quieter nearby. That doesn't prove none exists (SONYC and "quiet routes" projects weren't checked), but nothing in the mainstream does it.

## Conclusion

The case for Quiet Compass doesn't rest on noise being bad. It rests on a mismatch. The need is acute, recurring and local: someone at Moscone or in a Mission café hits overload and needs a quieter spot within minutes. The information that exists is either a crowd proxy, a static label, a past reading or a fixed room somewhere else. The Chronicle finding is the sharpest proof that the mismatch is structural. If a professional critic with a meter couldn't pin down one room's loudness for one night, a stored label can't either, and only a fresh, timestamped reading can. The MacLennan result about concerts being easier because they are predictable suggests the product's value may come as much from reducing uncertainty as from finding silence.

The evidence also shows where the pitch is exposed. The strongest impact numbers are British charity surveys, the US employment data is a decade old, and SF's dramatic complaint growth is partly an artifact of a better reporting app. A pitch that leads with the peer-reviewed prevalence data, uses the SF data as "reported complaints," and puts the information gap at the center will hold up to a skeptical judge. A pitch that claims SF got twice as loud or that 85% of autistic adults are unemployed will not.
